I run a small company in Las Vegas. The grant pitches that reach me are confident. The databases are not. A grant is money you do not repay, if a funder picks you. The picking is the whole game.

## Open the government site, not the ad

[Grants.gov](https://www.grants.gov/) is the federal list. Read eligibility before the award size. A lot of those programs are for nonprofits, schools, tribes, or state agencies. If the line does not include a shop like yours, it is not your grant, no matter how round the dollar figure is.

[SBA.gov](https://www.sba.gov/funding-programs) explains funding in plainer English, and a lot of it is loans. A loan is money you pay back. If the page blurs that, leave.

In Nevada I also check the [Small Business Development Center](https://nevadasbdc.org/) and the [Governor's Office of Economic Development](https://goed.nv.gov/). Local programs rarely show up in a national ad. Search the office name plus "grant" and click the .gov or .edu result.

## Four lines, from the official page

Copy these before you get excited:

- Who can apply.
- What the money can be used for.
- The deadline.
- Whether they want a match, meaning cash you put in.

A passed deadline is not a lead. If the page will not name the agency, find the agency yourself. Programs move. The tab you saved in March can be wrong in November.

## What I will sell, and what I will not

A list is useful when it saves that first pass: official link, deadline as printed that day, and the eligibility line that made it look close. It is useless when it says you will win.

The [grant match](/services/grant-match-report) I sell is $149. I email it within 48 hours of the form. I do not apply. I do not take a cut of any award. "Appears eligible" means the published rules look like a fit from what you told me. The funder still decides.

If the email says the money is already reserved and you just need to pay a fee today, read [how to spot a grant scam](/money/how-to-spot-a-small-business-grant-scam) before you pay me or anyone else.
