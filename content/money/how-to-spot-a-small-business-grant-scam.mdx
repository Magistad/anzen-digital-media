The pitch is usually short. You have been selected. The grant is guaranteed. You need to pay a fee today so they can release it. That is not how a real grant works.

A real program posts rules on an official site. You apply. Someone reviews it. Many applicants are turned down. If money comes, it comes from the agency, not from a stranger who needs your bank login.

## Stop signs

Treat these as reasons to close the tab.

Someone contacts you first, by text, email, or a social message, to say you won a grant you never applied for.

They guarantee approval, or they say the money is reserved for you.

They charge a fee up front to release, insure, or process money you have not received. A government agency does not ask you to pay a processing fee by wire, gift card, or cryptocurrency.

They want a percentage of the award, especially before you have an award letter from the agency.

The site is a lookalike. Federal programs live on .gov addresses such as [grants.gov](https://www.grants.gov/) and [sba.gov](https://www.sba.gov/). A .com page that copies an agency seal is not the agency. Type the address yourself. Do not trust a link in the message.

They rush you. A deadline on an official posting is normal. A stranger who says the offer expires tonight if you do not pay them is not.

They tell you not to contact the agency, your bank, or a Small Business Development Center.

## What to do instead

Do not pay. Do not send a Social Security number, a bank password, or a photo of your ID to a sender you did not seek out. Open the agency site yourself and see if the program exists.

You can report an impersonation to the Federal Trade Commission at [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). Your state attorney general takes these reports too.

A paid researcher can still be legitimate when the offer is narrow: a list of real programs, with official links, and no promise that you will be funded.
